You are analyzing an integration project built on a data integration platform. Your task is to produce a **Business Logic Detection** -- an inference of the business domains, extraction of business rules from all sources (transformation expressions, custom code, SQL queries, filter conditions, job naming patterns), and an impact analysis of what would break if these rules changed.

## Integration Platform Context
Business logic in integration projects is distributed across multiple layers, making it hard to find and document:
- **Transformation expressions**: Column-level transformations that often encode business rules. For example, `input.Status == "A" ? "Active" : "Inactive"` encodes a status code mapping that is a business rule.
- **Transformation filter expressions**: Row-level filters that determine which data passes through. For example, `input.OrderDate > parseDate("2020-01-01")` encodes a business cutoff date.
- **SQL WHERE clauses**: In source queries, WHERE conditions often encode business filters. For example, `WHERE IsDeleted = 0 AND Status IN ('A','P')` encodes which records are considered "active" by the business.
- **Custom code routines**: Custom methods (Java, Python, SQL functions, etc.) often contain the most complex business logic -- validation rules, calculation formulas, categorization logic, masking rules.
- **Inline code components**: Inline code blocks in components may contain conditional business logic, calculations, and data validation.
- **Job orchestration order**: The sequence in which jobs run (RAW -> CLEAN -> PUBLISH) often reflects a business process (ingest -> validate/transform -> make available).
- **Naming conventions**: Job and component names often encode business domain information. For example, `BRA_SALES_RAW` might mean "Brazil, Sales domain, raw data layer".
- **Configuration variable names**: Variables like `fiscal_year_start`, `cutoff_date`, `max_retry_count` indicate business parameters.
- **Schema column names**: Column names in schemas often use business terminology that reveals domain concepts.
- **Lookup joins**: A lookup join in a transformation often implements a business relationship (e.g., joining orders to customers, products to categories).
- **Aggregations**: Aggregation components implement business metrics (total sales, customer counts, average order value).

## What to Extract

1. **Business domain inference**: Based on job names, table names, column names, and data patterns, infer:
   - What business domains are covered (sales, finance, HR, manufacturing, logistics, etc.)?
   - What entities are being processed (orders, customers, products, invoices, employees, etc.)?
   - What business processes are being automated (daily sales sync, monthly financial close, customer data replication, etc.)?
   - What geographic or organizational units are involved (countries, business units, departments)?

2. **Business rules from transformation expressions**: For every transformation expression that encodes a business rule:
   - The raw expression
   - Plain-English business rule description
   - The business domain it belongs to
   - Confidence level (CERTAIN, LIKELY, INFERRED) -- is this clearly a business rule or a technical transformation?
   - Data affected

3. **Business rules from SQL queries**: For every SQL query with business-meaningful WHERE, JOIN, or CASE logic:
   - The relevant SQL fragment
   - What business filter or rule it implements
   - What data is included/excluded and why

4. **Business rules from custom code**: For every piece of custom code containing business logic:
   - The code snippet
   - The business rule in plain English
   - Whether this rule is also documented elsewhere or only exists in code

5. **Business rules from job structure**: Infer rules from the overall job architecture:
   - Data layer patterns (RAW/STAGING/CLEAN/PUBLISH) and what each layer does to the data
   - Job execution order and what business process it mirrors
   - Conditional job execution and what business conditions control it

6. **Business parameter catalog**: List every value that appears to be a business parameter:
   - Hardcoded dates (fiscal year boundaries, migration cutoffs, go-live dates)
   - Hardcoded status codes and their meanings
   - Hardcoded thresholds (minimum order amounts, maximum retry counts, batch sizes that reflect business SLAs)
   - Category mappings and lookup values
   - Default values used when data is missing

7. **Impact analysis**: For each identified business rule, assess:
   - What jobs implement this rule?
   - What downstream data would change if the rule changed?
   - What external systems consume the affected data?
   - Estimated blast radius: how many tables, reports, or processes would be affected?
   - Is the rule implemented in one place or duplicated across multiple jobs?

8. **Business rule conflicts**: Identify cases where:
   - The same business concept is handled differently in different jobs
   - Status code mappings are inconsistent
   - Date format assumptions differ
   - Filter conditions on the same entity differ between jobs

## Instructions

- **Be thorough.** Business rules are the most critical documentation output. Every transformation expression, every SQL WHERE clause, every code if-statement should be examined for business meaning.
- If you are uncertain whether something is a business rule or a technical transformation, include it with confidence level INFERRED and let the reader decide.
- Business rule conflicts are high-value findings -- inconsistencies between regions or domains often indicate bugs or undocumented business requirements.
- Hardcoded business parameters (dates, status codes, thresholds) are maintenance risks. Always recommend parameterization.
- Impact analysis should trace the full downstream blast radius of each rule -- from the job where it is implemented to the final consumers of the data.
